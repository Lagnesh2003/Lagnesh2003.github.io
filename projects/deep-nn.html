Deep learning from scratch, through world models

I wrote the core of deep learning by hand, checked each piece against PyTorch, and applied all of it to one robotics problem: a learned dynamics model (world model) of the Furuta pendulum from saferl-lab. The same model is the base for model-based RL on the real pendulum.

A Furuta pendulum world model

Input: the current observation (7 numbers) and the motor command. Output: the change in state over one 10 ms step. Besides one-step error I measure the 50-step rollout error, where the model feeds on its own predictions. Small one-step errors that pile up over a rollout are the real problem in world models.

The training recipe (AdamW, 5% warmup + cosine decay, weight decay, batch 256) came from experiments on optimizers, schedules, clipping, regularization and a 20-trial random search. Over 5 seeds the tuned recipe beat the default 0.00090 ± 0.00007 vs 0.00307 ± 0.00022 validation MSE, 21 standard errors apart; only the learning rate really mattered.

Key finding

10× more data left the hardest output (pendulum speed) unchanged with the MLP; the data-scaling curve had flattened. The 4.2M-parameter SimBa model cut that error 4× in one step: the model was the bottleneck, not the data. At equal size, SimBa is still 1.9× better than an MLP, so the gain is architecture, not just parameters.

Sequence models: when the robot only measures angles

The real pendulum's encoders measure angles, not speeds. So the speeds are removed from the input and the model must infer them from history. I compared an MLP, SimBa, LSTM and causal Transformer at about 270k parameters, under clean sensing, encoder noise, a 20 ms delay and a hidden ±30% mass change.

A result I had to retract

My first comparison said “the Transformer beats the LSTM 2×”. Every model had used a learning rate of 1e-3. Giving each model its own tuned rate (10–30× higher) improved the LSTM 6× and overturned the result. One-step rank also did not predict rollouts: the one-step winner was about 2.3× worse than SimBa at step 50.

A second look then made the Transformer modern (RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU), and it gave the best 50-step rollouts of the angle-only models: 0.0219 vs 0.0277 for the LSTM and 0.0285 for SimBa. Under a 20 ms delay it was barely hurt (0.0222), while SimBa got 2.5× worse. It is now the default for the angle-only world model. Training was also made 1.8–5.1× faster with identical results (fused Adam, fused attention, CUDA graphs, TF32).

A world model that reports its uncertainty

A Gaussian head (mean + σ) on the modern Transformer, trained with MSE, Gaussian NLL and β-NLL (β = 0.5) over 3 seeds. β-NLL is the right loss: calibrated σ (zrms 0.98 on clean data) with a mean almost as good as MSE, while plain NLL made the mean 17× worse.

On changed physics (mass 1.5–1.8×) the error grew 8× but an ensemble's epistemic σ grew only 8%. The inputs look familiar, so the members agree. An ensemble cannot detect changed dynamics; that needs an online surprise check against reality. This matters directly for a safety filter on the real robot.

Other lessons, each from a measured experiment

  • Initialization: in a 20-layer ReLU net, N(0,1) overflows to NaN, Xavier shrinks activations to 2e-4, He keeps them flat (val 0.013 vs 0.057).
  • BatchNorm in RL: on consecutive, RL-like batches it gets 5× worse and cannot run on a single state; LayerNorm and RMSNorm do not have this problem.
  • Precision: in bf16, 1.0 − 0.0001 stays 1.0, so all 1000 small updates are lost; autocast gave 1.9× speed at width 2048 with no accuracy change over 5 seeds.
  • Debugging: five planted bugs, each identified from its loss curve alone; two of them look identical and only a shape assert tells them apart.
